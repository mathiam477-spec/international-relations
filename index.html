Professor's Exam Guidelines

Exam scope: Weeks 1 to 6 (Exam postponed to Week 6).

Crucial readings: Baylis (Ch. 3,4,5,6 - Historical Context) and the London chapter on Power.

Core Quote: "International relations is everything about power."

Core Concepts

IR is not just "how countries communicate", but how and why international actors interact with each other. It is directly linked to International Business (geopolitical due diligence).

Definition: International Actor

An actor capable of influencing international relations. International actors = States + Non-state actors.

  • Nation-States: Primary actors (sign treaties, trade, war).
  • Non-State Actors: Multinational Corporations (MNCs), International Organizations, Individuals.

Levels of Analysis

To understand why a country behaves a certain way, look at three levels:

  • Systemic / International level: Distribution of power, alliances, system structure.
  • Domestic level: Political system, institutions, internal economy.
  • Individual level: Leaders and their decisions.

Exam Key Takeaways

  • Anarchy: There is no overarching global authority. It does NOT mean "total chaos".
  • Geopolitics: Relationship between geography, power, and political interests.
  • Diplomacy: A tool to shape international behavior (via aid, trade, sanctions).

Crucial Exam Definition: International Order

"Regularized practices of exchange among discrete political units that recognize each other to be independent."

Interdependence & The Security Dilemma

Interaction creates Interdependence ("I have what you need. You have what I need. Let's trade!").

Liberal Logic: Interdependence → Cooperation → Harmony of interests → Higher cost of conflict → Lower incentives for war.

Exam Trap: Interdependence does NOT guarantee peace. (e.g., UK/Germany before WWI, US/China today). It only reduces incentives.
Realist Logic (Security Dilemma): State A builds power to survive → State B feels threatened and builds arms → Arms Race → Potential Conflict.
"It's better to be Godzilla than to be Bambi." (Weak states must seek powerful allies).

From Westphalia to Today

  • Peace of Westphalia (1648): The birth of the modern Nation-State system.
  • System Evolution: Moved from regional orders to a global system driven by technology and globalization.
  • Current State: The Western-led / Rules-Based / Liberal International Order is currently in a systemic crisis due to multipolarization and Great Power Competition (US vs. China).

Historical Milestones

  • 1919 (University of Wales): Birth of IR as a discipline. First scholars were "Idealists" trying to prevent war.
  • League of Nations: Failed to prevent WWII.
  • Treaty of Versailles (1919) & Great Depression (1929): Harsh reparations led to nationalism and WWII (Example of International Political Economy).
  • 1945: United Nations replaces League; Bretton Woods establishes global economic governance.

The Cold War & Containment

During the Cold War, there was ONE global system, but TWO competing International Orders (Western vs. Communist).

Containment (Truman Doctrine): Strategy to prevent the expansion of an adversary's power (Communism). Rooted in Realism.

Post-1991: Collapse of USSR led to Unipolarity (US dominance) and the expansion of the Rules-Based Order. The Rise of the Rest (China, India) has now created a Multipolar world.

The Concept of Power

Definition: The ability to influence the behavior of others.

  • 🔥 Hard Power: Influence via coercion (military, economic sanctions).
  • 🌊 Soft Power (J. Nye): Influence via attraction, values, culture ("lead by example").
  • ⚖️ Institutional Power: Shaping the rules of the game.
  • 📊 Relative Power vs Absolute Power: Relative power (your power compared to others) is what truly matters in IR.

Polarity: How power is distributed.

Bipolarity (Cold War) → Unipolarity (1990s) → Multipolarity (Today).

Liberals accept that the system is anarchic, but believe this anarchy can be organized through Interdependence, Cooperation, and Institutions.

Interdependence → Shared Interests → Cooperation → International Regimes → Peace

International Regimes

A set of principles, norms, rules, decision-making procedures, and organizations that organize international cooperation. They help solve transnational problems (climate, economy) that single states cannot solve alone.

Note: International law relies on State Consent. There is no world government for enforcement.

Democratic Peace Theory

Liberal democracies are less likely to go to war with each other (due to shared values and institutions). However, they can still go to war with non-democracies.

Exam note: This is an explanatory theory, NOT "Democracy promotion" (which is a foreign policy).

Key Thinkers

John Locke, Immanuel Kant, David Ricardo. (Also Francis Fukuyama - "End of History", though this text is optional for the exam).

Developed as a critique of Idealism post-WWII. Realism is primarily concerned with the persistence of conflict and war.

THE THREE S's OF REALISM

1. Statism

States are the most important actors. Sovereignty = Ultimate internal authority + No higher external authority.

2. Survival

The fundamental objective. Because of anarchy, survival cannot be guaranteed.

3. Self-Help

States must build their own power to survive; they cannot rely on others.

The Realist Chain of Conflict

Must know this logic for the exam:

1. Anarchy → 2. Insecurity → 3. Survival → 4. Self-help →
5. Power → 6. Security Dilemma → 7. Arms Race → 8. Conflict

Variants of Realism (Preview)

  • Classical Realism: Conflict is due to flawed Human Nature (Machiavelli, Hobbes, Morgenthau).
  • Neorealism (Structural): Conflict is due to the Anarchic System (Waltz, Mearsheimer).
  • Neoclassical Realism: Systemic anarchy + Domestic variables (leaders, institutions) (Zakaria).
