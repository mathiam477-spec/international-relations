International Relations

Revision Notes

Summary of key concepts from your presentations.

1. Fundamental Concepts (State, Nation, Actors)

The State

An organized political entity exercising sovereignty over a territory and its population. Defined by the Montevideo Convention (1933) according to 4 criteria:

  • A permanent population
  • A defined territory
  • A government (supreme internal authority)
  • The capacity to enter into relations with other states (external sovereignty/diplomatic recognition)

Nation and Nation-State

A Nation is an "imagined community" (Benedict Anderson) sharing culture, language, or history.
A Nation-State combines this common identity within fixed borders with a single government. Conversely, a stateless nation lacks its own government (e.g., Kurds, Palestinians).

Non-State Actors

International relations are not limited to states. Other actors include:

  • IGOs (Intergovernmental Organizations): UN, WTO, NATO (created by states).
  • NGOs & MNCs (Multinational Corporations): Operate across borders, influencing the economy and norms (e.g., Apple, Standard Oil).
  • Individuals/Intrastate groups: Social movements, armed groups, political leaders.

2. Realism in IR

"We have no eternal allies, and we have no perpetual enemies. Our interests are eternal and perpetual, and those interests it is our duty to follow." - Lord Palmerston

The 3 Core Elements (3 S's)

  1. Statism: The state is the pre-eminent actor. Sovereignty is key.
  2. Survival: The primary goal. In an anarchic system, politics is a struggle for power to ensure survival. Universal morality does not apply (Raison d'État / Realpolitik).
  3. Self-help: No other state can guarantee your security. This creates the "Security Dilemma".

Variants of Realism

  • Classical Realism (Thucydides, Machiavelli, Hobbes, Morgenthau): The source of conflict is human nature, which is inherently selfish and power-seeking.
  • Neorealism / Structural Realism (Kenneth Waltz, Mearsheimer): The source of conflict is the anarchic international system, not human nature.
    - Defensive (Waltz): States seek moderate security. Bipolarity is stable.
    - Offensive (Mearsheimer): States must seek hegemony for ultimate survival ("better to be Godzilla than Bambi").
  • Neoclassical Realism (Zakaria, Rose): Combines systemic anarchy with domestic variables (leaders' perceptions, state institutions).

3. Liberalism in IR

Foundations: Locke, Kant, Adam Smith. Optimistic view of human nature (rationality, freedom). War is a failure of cooperation; peace must be built.

Key Principles

  • Interdependence & Cooperation: Trade and shared interests reduce the risk of war (positive-sum game).
  • International Institutions: Regimes, laws, and IGOs (UN, WTO) bring order, reduce uncertainty, and foster cooperation.
  • The State is NOT a Unitary Actor: Foreign policy reflects the interests of various domestic groups (bureaucracies, lobbies, public opinion).

Democratic Peace Theory

Inspired by Immanuel Kant ("Perpetual Peace"). Premise: Liberal democratic states almost never go to war with each other because they share values, and citizens (who bear the cost of war) constrain aggressive leaders.

4. Levels of Analysis & Historical Context

The 3 Levels of Analysis

  • System Level (The Forest): Anarchy, distribution of power, polarity (unipolar, bipolar, multipolar). Primary focus of Neorealism.
  • State Level (The Tree): Type of government, economy, public opinion, interest groups. Focus of Liberalism.
  • Individual Level (Inside): Psychology of leaders, bounded rationality, beliefs, health, perceptions.

Historical Evolution & Globalization

Westphalia (1648): Birth of the modern order based on territorial sovereignty and non-intervention.
Bretton Woods (1944): Creation of IMF and World Bank for economic stability.
Globalization vs. Deglobalization (Slowbalization): According to realists, globalization is epiphenomenal (subordinate to geopolitics). States ultimately retain control, as seen in trade wars and sanctions.
