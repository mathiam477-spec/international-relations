1. Core Concepts, Actors & Power

State, Nation, and Sovereignty

Anarchy is the ordering principle of the international system (no central overriding authority). Within this system, the main units are states.

  • The State: An organized political entity defined by the Montevideo Convention (1933) with 4 criteria: A permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states.
  • Nation & Nation-State: A Nation is an "imagined community" (Benedict Anderson) sharing culture, language, or history. A Nation-State combines this identity with fixed borders and a government.
  • Stateless Nation: A group with a common identity but lacking their own sovereign government (e.g., Kurds, Palestinians).
  • Sovereignty vs. Independence: Independence is formal; sovereignty is the actual supreme authority (internal control and external recognition). E.g., Iraq after 2003 was independent but had limited sovereignty due to US occupation.

International Actors

  • Interstate Governmental Actors (IGOs): Created by states (UN, NATO, WTO).
  • Interstate Nongovernmental Actors: Multinational Corporations (MNCs like Apple) and NGOs. MNCs promote globalization and control vast economic power.
  • Other Actors: Governmental Noncentral Actors (regions/cities), Intrastate Armed/Social groups, and Individuals (leaders).

Power and Polarity

Power is relative and relational (the ability to influence others). It takes several forms:

  • Hard Power (Sticks/Carrots): Military force or economic pressure/sanctions.
  • Soft Power (Joseph Nye): The ability to attract and co-opt using cultural influence, political values, and legitimate policies (e.g., the "American Way of Life").
  • Smart Power: Strategic combination of both hard and soft power.

Polarity refers to the distribution of power (Unipolar, Bipolar during Cold War, Multipolar).

2. Historical Context & Globalization

The Making of Modern Order

  • Peace of Westphalia (1648): Ended the 30 Years' War. Established the legal basis of modern world politics: Territoriality, Sovereignty, and Autonomy (non-intervention).
  • The Great Divergence & Imperialism: 1800-1900 saw the West drastically outpace Asia in GDP due to industrialization, "rational states," and imperialism (e.g., the British Empire).
  • World Wars & Institutions: WWI led to the Treaty of Versailles and the failed League of Nations. WWII led to the United Nations (1945) and the Bretton Woods System (1944) which created the IMF, World Bank, and set the dollar-gold standard.

The Cold War & Beyond

  • Cold War (1947-1991): Bipolar tension. Characterized by the Truman Doctrine (containment of communism), Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD), and crises like the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962). Ended with Gorbachev's reforms (Glasnost/Perestroika) and the fall of the USSR.
  • Post-Cold War Era: The "War on Terror" (post-9/11/2001), the Arab Spring (2011), the rise of BRICS demanding global governance reform, and renewed conflict (Russia/Ukraine, US/China trade wars).

Globalization vs. Slowbalization

The "Golden Age" of globalization (1990-2010) saw massive trade and integration. However, recent events (2008 financial crisis, COVID-19, US-China trade wars) have led to Slowbalization or even Deglobalization. Realists view globalization as epiphenomenal—subordinate to underlying geopolitical power struggles.

3. Realism in IR

"We have no eternal allies, and we have no perpetual enemies. Our interests are eternal and perpetual." - Lord Palmerston

The 3 Core Elements (3 S's) & Realpolitik

Driven by Realpolitik (practical/material considerations over morals) and Raison d'État (national survival above all).

  1. Statism: The state is the pre-eminent actor. Sovereignty is absolute.
  2. Survival: The primary goal. Universal morality does not apply to states.
  3. Self-help: In anarchy, no other state can guarantee your security, creating a Security Dilemma.

Thinkers & Variants of Realism

  • Classical Realism (Human Nature): Rooted in the flawed, power-seeking nature of humans.
    Thinkers: Thucydides ("the strong do what they can..."), Machiavelli (better to be feared than loved), Hobbes (war of all against all), Hans Morgenthau (morality cannot be applied to state actions).
  • Neorealism / Structural Realism (Anarchy): The anarchic system causes conflict, not human nature.
    Defensive (Kenneth Waltz): States seek moderate security; bipolarity is most stable.
    Offensive (John Mearsheimer): States must pursue hegemony to survive ("better to be Godzilla than Bambi"). Mearsheimer notably blames NATO expansion for the Ukraine crisis.
  • Neoclassical Realism: Combines systemic anarchy with domestic variables (leaders' perceptions, elites).
    Thinkers: Fareed Zakaria, Gideon Rose.

4. Liberalism in IR

Core Beliefs: Optimistic view of human nature. Rationality and freedom are inherent. War is a failure of cooperation; peace must be constructed.

Key Thinkers & Origins

  • Hugo Grotius & Samuel Pufendorf: Early concepts of Natural Law and international sociability.
  • John Locke: Government based on social contract; natural rights (life, liberty, property).
  • Immanuel Kant: Author of Perpetual Peace. Proposed a federation of free republics.
  • Woodrow Wilson: Driving force behind the League of Nations (Liberal Internationalism).
  • Francis Fukuyama: Declared the "End of History" (1992) — the ultimate triumph of Western liberal democracy post-Cold War.

Key Principles & Democratic Peace

  • Interdependence & Trade: Economic ties reduce the incentive for war (positive-sum game).
  • Democratic Peace Theory: Democracies rarely fight each other due to shared values and domestic constraints (citizens bear the cost of war).
  • State is NOT Unitary: Foreign policy is a competition among internal bureaucracies, lobbies, and public opinion.

Neoliberalism & International Regimes

Neoliberal institutionalists (like Keohane and Nye) argue that while the system is anarchic, International Regimes (rules, norms, and institutions like the WTO or Non-Proliferation treaties) allow states to coordinate, reduce uncertainty, and collaborate effectively without a global government.

5. Levels of Analysis

To understand complex world politics, IR uses three levels to determine which facts are central to the analysis:

  • System Level (The Forest): Looks at the system as a whole. Focuses on the distribution of power (polarity), global anarchy, and interactions between states. (Primary focus of Neorealism and Neoliberalism).
  • State / Domestic Level (The Tree): Looks inside the state. Focuses on government structure (democracy vs. authoritarian), economy, public opinion, and interest groups. E.g., How Portugal's Estado Novo regime delayed African decolonization. (Primary focus of Liberalism).
  • Individual Level (The Roots): Focuses on specific human beings. Examines the psychology of leaders, bounded rationality, personal beliefs, health, and groupthink dynamics.
